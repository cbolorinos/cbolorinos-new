Summit App

Client: WSA → independent product

Role: Product Design Lead / Co-Founder

60% attendee adoptionEngagement up to 2 months post-eventWhite-label app

Post-event interviews kept surfacing the same three pains: unnavigable PDF calendars, inefficient networking, and a community that fragmented after every event. The Summit App fixed all three. Over 60% of attendees used it, and it continues getting engagement up to two months after events.

Context

WSA is a UN-backed organization that gives awards to projects supporting the UN Sustainable Development Goals. Several times a year, they host regional and global events to celebrate award winners and provide valuable networking opportunities for attendees. I was in charge of the WSA website (there is another case study about that). To make the website more helpful, I often followed up with 5–10 people I had met at each event. I asked them what they were hoping to get out of the event and what could be improved.

Problem

In these interviews several recurring themes came up over and over again. One was that the calendar (a PDF sent through a WhatsApp group) was hard to navigate. Another was the inefficiency of the networking. A person working in agritech in Japan had very little to say to someone working in fintech in Chile. Also, the jumble of emails and LinkedIn connections attendees ended up with was often difficult to sort through and follow up on. The WSA community would come together for these events, but would mostly fragment again immediately afterwards.

What I did

My first impulse was to address these issues through the website. I built several MVPs, including an AI agent that had access to attendee and project data and suggested connections aligned with each user’s goals. These solutions received mostly positive feedback but were clunky and often drowned out by other communication channels. I also evaluated several SaaS solutions. While many solved the calendar problem, none effectively improved networking or helped maintain community engagement after the event. I considered building a custom app, but WSA did not have an in-house mobile developer or the budget to hire an agency.

Team and constraints

Summit was built by two people for one very clear stakeholder. Emeka, a developer I met through the WSA community, built the app and worked partly for equity. Making it white-label, so it could serve clients beyond WSA, was his idea, and it’s the reason Summit is an independent product today. Peter Bruck, WSA’s Chairman, was only interested in an app if it kept people engaged between events and strengthened what WSA really offers its members: network capital. That set the bar for every decision. A better calendar alone wouldn’t meet it, so the calendar was only the first iteration. The second focused on projects, the community feed and connections.

The Summit App

Explore it on the Summit website.

The first iteration of the app focused solely on calendar functionality. It provided a clear schedule of all sessions, allowed users to sync events with their personal calendars (iCal, Outlook, or Google), view session locations via Google Maps or Apple Maps, and access speaker profiles and LinkedIn pages. The app was embedded within the website so that users who preferred not to download an app could still access the schedule. This successfully solved the calendar problem but did not yet address networking. However, it helped onboard users, which made later iterations easier to roll out.

The second iteration retained the calendar and introduced two new sections: Projects and Community Feed. The Projects section displayed profiles of all winning teams, their objectives, and their social links, allowing attendees to follow up after the event. The Community Feed functioned as a forum where participants could post what they were looking for (investors, employees, or partnerships) and discover others with similar goals. The app also allowed users to browse attendee profiles and identify who might be most relevant to connect with.

The app also featured an AI agent that could provide information about winners, the agenda, and attendees. This agent supported networking during the event by suggesting people to connect with based on each user’s expertise and objectives. It also helped attendees use their time more efficiently by recommending the most relevant sessions.

One of the big problems that we anticipated with the app was it getting spammed once it got in the wrong hands. We solved this in the onboarding flow. When users were creating an account they are asked for their email. This is standard practice, for the email confirmation, but we also used this step to check against an admins email list. Admins could upload CSVs of their mailing list, which would serve as the list of approved emails. If the email was on the admin list, the user would be given full access to the app. If they weren't they were given guest access, which didn't let them post to the forum or access the directory of other guests. They could also get full access by getting an invite code (these were given out on email marketing campaigns or displayed on QR codes at events), but would expire after an allotted time.

Since the app was white-label, we had to separate which parts of the branding were customizeable (the colors, fonts, logo, and some of the wording, and features that could be turned on or off) from the parts that weren't (the glassy textures, the gradients, and the basic functionality). This meant the design library had to be built in a very specific way to accomodate these kinds of changes, and the code had to be treated similarly, with one file being customizeable, so as to be able to change the appearance entirely when switched out.

Results
60%attendee adoption

After two events the data was clear: over 60% of attendees engaged with the app, and it continues getting engagement up to two months after events, implying real follow-ups.

That second number is the one Peter cared about: people were still using the app to follow up long after the event ended.

Summit has since spun out of WSA and now operates as an independent product, serving clients with no connection to the organisation it was originally built for — among them Gomde and Quinta del Sordo.

WSA Country Pages

Client: WSA

Role: Product Design Lead

19% of all users visited pages within 3 monthsStandardized application process

Applicants had to track down their country’s local expert just to learn how to apply. I designed and built dynamic country pages that centralized everything in one place. Within three months, 19% of all users were visiting them and application volume rose significantly.

Context

WSA is a global initiative supporting digital projects aligned with the UN Sustainable Development Goals. Because any UN member state can participate, the organization operates through a distributed network of local coordinators who manage country-specific application processes and community engagement.

Problem

On the previous website, this created significant friction for applicants. Users had to manually find their country’s local expert and contact them to understand the application process, which varied widely between regions. As a result, potential applicants often struggled to understand how to participate or how to engage with their local WSA community.

What I did

To address this, I designed and developed a system of dynamic country pages that centralized all local information in one place. Each page displayed the country-specific application process, linked directly to the correct submission database, and showcased local winners, youth ambassadors, national experts, and upcoming events. The pages followed a standard treatment to stay consistent and maintainable globally, but were also customizable by the local experts to reflect each country’s process. This allowed users to quickly understand how to apply and provided clear entry points into their local WSA network.

Team and constraints

There was no in-house development team. Stefan Rath, the developer who had built the original WSA site, was on a four-hours-a-month contract, so I designed the pages and wrote most of the code myself. Stefan stepped in on bugs I couldn’t crack, and helped me connect the pages to a country ID that already existed in the database, so national experts and projects would be linked to the right country automatically. I worked with WSA’s community manager to map how the application process differed between countries, so the system reflected local processes while staying maintainable across every country.

Results
19%of all site users

Within three months of launch, 19% of users were visiting the country pages. Application volume rose significantly, WSA’s local presence strengthened, and users could move from discovery to application in a single flow.

Beyond the country pages

In addition to the country pages, I worked on improving regional engagement through WSA’s newsletter. Using location data available in Brevo, I segmented WSA’s 20,000-contact mailing list into five geographic regions. This allowed us to promote local events and highlight regionally relevant success stories, which led to improved engagement and higher open rates in subsequent campaigns.

I also produced an explainer video for WSA, creating motion graphics that helped communicate the organization’s mission and participation model more clearly to new audiences.

Ria Interface Design

Client: Ria Money Transfer

Role: UI & Digital Designer

Simultaneous launch in 5 marketsLottie animations at friction momentsProduct ↔ support feedback loop

Ria’s mobile app had to launch across five markets at once, in the middle of a pandemic. I was in charge of enforcing the newly defined brand guidelines and doing some custom illustrations and animations. I also organized the feedback sessions between product and customer support that helped inform product direction.

Context

Before I was involved in the Ria app, I had spent the better part of a year building the internal infrastructure for Ria’s visual identity. This included a Digital Asset Management system that gave every team (developers, designers, comms, marketing, and brick-and-mortar operations) a single source of truth for brand assets. It also included an internal intranet and a comprehensive set of brand guidelines built in Frontify, which formalized Ria’s evolving visual language across touchpoints for the first time.

Problem

That groundwork is what got me looped into the app. Ria was preparing to launch its mobile app across multiple markets simultaneously, and the design team needed someone who understood the brand deeply enough to ensure the app’s visual language felt continuous with everything else Ria was putting into the world. The design challenge was one of translation. The warmth and cultural specificity of Ria’s social media presence, the visual codes of the physical locations, the illustration style we’d developed for marketing; all of it needed to find its equivalent in a mobile interface, which operates under very different constraints.

What I did

My contribution had a few distinct threads. I worked directly with the product team to introduce the illustration assets I had developed into the app, reformatting them for mobile dimensions and animating a selection in Lottie to guide users through key flows and improve comprehension at moments of friction. I also ran sessions showing the product team how to use the intranet and brand guidelines correctly.

Beyond the brief

The other thing I did wasn’t in my job description at all. Ria’s product team was based in Colorado. Customer support was in Madrid, which was where I worked. The two departments had almost no structured communication, and as a result product decisions were being made without a clear line of sight into the issues customers were actually experiencing. Having studied UX, I knew what that gap cost in terms of wasted iteration cycles and avoidable friction. So I organized the first monthly cross-department feedback meetings between the two teams. I ran the first sessions, and handed it off once it was established. The app launched as a critical continuity channel during the early months of the pandemic, when Ria’s physical network was severely disrupted, and those feedback loops proved essential to identifying and resolving friction points quickly under pressure.

Results
5markets at launch

The app launched across the US, Canada, UK, Spain and Australia, becoming a critical continuity channel while Ria’s physical network was disrupted by lockdowns.

5mins eLearning App

Client: 5Mins

Role: Product Designer (Engagement & Gamification)

+18% 7-day retention<1k → 7k+ monthly usersAI-generated assessments

As 5Mins scaled from less than 1k to more than 7k monthly users, repeat usage plateaued after onboarding. I was responsible for redesigning the engagement layer of the product, focusing on social interaction and gamification to encourage sustained learning behavior. My work increased 7-day retention by 18% and improved repeat usage across the platform.

Problem

When I joined, the home page primarily surfaced content automatically based on users’ skill sets. While this worked at smaller scale, it offered little visibility into team activity or shared learning.

What I did: social home experience

I redesigned the home experience to introduce a social feed inspired by familiar interaction patterns from TikTok and Instagram. The goal was to leverage interfaces users already understood, reducing the learning curve and allowing them to seamlessly browse, share, and discover learning content in a way that felt natural.

This included introducing a Share feature that allowed users to distribute videos within or outside the app, as well as a social feed that surfaced content suggested by coworkers, trending within teams, or assigned by managers. The decision to prioritize social features was also informed by broader platform history. As described by Tim Wu in his analysis of early internet platforms, services like AOL gained dominance not through content, but by embedding social interaction directly into the user experience. Applying this principle to 5Mins, we focused on making learning activity visible and shareable within teams, rather than treating it as an isolated task.

What I did: gamification system

The platform initially included basic streaks and daily goals, which proved insufficient for long-term motivation. I designed a structured learning path supported by badges and certificates, drawing inspiration from Duolingo’s progression and reward systems.

Users unlocked badges by earning skill points through watching relevant videos. After unlocking three badges, they became eligible to earn certificates. Upon completing the required lessons, users were prompted to take an assessment. Since every user watched different videos, there was no way to automatically generate quizzes, as each learning path was unique. To solve this, we included an AI agent powered by ChatGPT. It analyzed their recent lessons and generated quiz questions. Users scoring above 80% received a shareable certificate, meaning they could demonstrate tangible proof of their learnings on LinkedIn.

Team and constraints

I reported to the PM, whose priority was the KPIs shown to investors. The company depended on its next funding round, so retention and engagement numbers were the brief. The developers were overstretched, so every design had to move those numbers without adding much build time.

That made the design system a constant negotiation between two sources of truth: my Figma library and the developers’ Storybook. Rather than forcing one to always win, I reconciled them component by component. Where Storybook had drifted on brand consistency or information hierarchy (inconsistent CTAs, mixed font usage, mismatched corner radii), we brought Storybook back in line with Figma, because those inconsistencies erode trust in the interface. Where the developers’ version of a component simplified the app’s logic, I adapted Figma to Storybook. Design and code stayed in sync, and the developers didn’t have to rebuild anything that already worked.

Results
+18%7-day retention

The redesigned engagement layer lifted 7-day retention by 18% and improved repeat usage across the platform as 5Mins scaled past 7k monthly users.

SwissBorg Website

Client: SwissBorg

Role: Growth Product Designer

2x website conversion rate+20% downloads from one A/B test-25% social acquisition costs

At SwissBorg, I led initiatives to optimize the marketing website, improve app downloads, and validate new product offerings through UX research and experimentation.

Context

I joined SwissBorg on a 6 month contract to work on conversion, and everything below was designed, tested and shipped inside that window.

I had a lot of autonomy. The PM’s goals were simple: more app downloads and more premium upsells, and how to get there was up to me. I proposed running A/B tests; the developers already had the infrastructure, so it was easy to start. I also set up Hotjar, so we could see which interactive components people actually used, not just which variant won.

What I did

The website’s main goal was to drive app downloads. I designed and executed conversion optimization initiatives. One of these was running continuous A/B tests, and prioritizing high-performing components. One clear example was the QR code on the home screen: originally visible only on hover, displaying it by default led to an increase in the conversion rate by about 20%.

Beyond the website itself, I wanted to know which of SwissBorg’s services converted best, so I asked. Nobody had an answer. So I designed a social media campaign where each ad featured a different service while every other variable was held constant: video length, time of day, the template used to make the videos, and so on. The results informed prioritization of offerings and contributed to a 25% reduction in social media marketing costs for the same results. It also showed us which services needed to be featured more prominently on the website.

I also optimized the premium upsell experience on the website. Using interactive yield comparison graphs, we highlighted subscription plan differences and token-based rewards (CHSB) while maintaining clarity for users of varying expertise. Hotjar data helped identify which interactive components drove conversions, and an accordion pattern allowed users to access complex information without cluttering the main experience.

Results
2xconversion rate

Continuous A/B testing and experimentation doubled the website’s conversion rate during my time at SwissBorg, while social campaign experiments cut acquisition costs by 25% for prioritized offerings.

Barclay Damon Website

Role: Product Designer

9 offices · 65 practice areasHundreds of attorneysStill live ~10 years later

This project focused on redesigning the website for Barclay Damon, an international law firm with 9 offices, 65 practice areas, and hundreds of attorneys. The core challenge was not visual refresh, but scale: creating a system that could accommodate a large, complex organization without fragmenting the brand or overwhelming users.

What I did

Early in the process, I explored multiple strategic directions for how the firm could present itself digitally. One concept emphasized Barclay Damon’s Renewable Energy practice, while another used a more abstract, flexible visual system capable of representing a wide range of legal domains. These explorations were used to align internal stakeholders around what the website needed to do for the business.

Team and constraints

At Saturno I worked between a PM from a design background, who pushed for beautiful, current, craft-driven work, and a small dev team who pushed back for simplicity and reduced dev time.

Results
10 yrsand still live

The site was built almost 10 years ago, is still live, and still doesn’t look dated — the modular system has absorbed a decade of new pages without fragmenting.

While my role included hands-on design, the strategic value of the project lay in translating organizational complexity into a coherent digital structure that balanced marketing needs, editorial autonomy, and long-term maintainability within a custom CMS environment. It remains one of my favorite examples of my early work!

Ria Money Transfer Corporate Communications

Client: Ria Money Transfer

Role: Digital Designer/Brand Strategist

+200% Instagram+70% LinkedInMost-followed brand in its competitive set

Walk past a Ria location in most European or American cities and you’ll find it wedged between a shop selling burner phones and a stall flogging counterfeit sneakers. That association (low-rent, cash-in-hand, the last resort of the unbanked) was Ria’s brand problem in a nutshell.
Meanwhile, a new generation of fintech darlings (Wise, Revolut, Remitly) were building glossy digital identities and becoming household names in tech circles, despite serving a fraction of the markets Ria quietly dominated.

Problem

The truth was quite the opposite. Ria was one of the largest money transfer operators in the world, moving billions of dollars a year to countries and corridors that Wise had never even considered. It was a lifeline for migrant families, a financial artery for communities in parts of Africa, Latin America, and Southeast Asia where most people had never heard of Revolut. My department’s mandate wasn’t marketing in the conventional sense. It was to change how the world saw Ria, starting with prospective talent and potential partners, and to make visible a story the company had never bothered to tell.

What I did

The strategic anchor was repositioning Ria as the fintech that served the bottom billion. This meant moving away from transactional messaging and toward storytelling that foregrounded the human and economic weight of what Ria actually did. One of the most effective executions of this was the Orange Envelope. This video followed the emotional journey of a remittance payment from a worker abroad to their family at home. It showed what the money meant to the recipients and how it could change their lives. On the other end of the spectrum, our Microfinance video educated audiences on a corner of financial systems that mainstream fintech commentary almost entirely ignores, and one where Ria had more operational depth and nuance than any of its better-known competitors.

Content was distributed across Instagram, LinkedIn, and YouTube, with topic selection driven by keyword research: we targeted concepts that were frequently searched but underrepresented in quality content. Measuring success, however, required more precision than follower counts or likes. We defined specific KPIs around LinkedIn engagement from decision-makers and senior roles at top fintech companies to track whether the people we actually wanted to reach were interacting with our content. This distinction mattered because any post mentioning Atlético de Madrid, for instance, would generate enormous engagement from football fans. Unfortunately, these posts almost never got engagement from the audience we were trying to influence. Filtering for role-based engagement gave us a much cleaner signal about what was resonating with fintech leaders specifically, and let us iterate content strategy based on data that was actually meaningful to our objectives.

Results
+200%Instagram following

LinkedIn grew by 70%, making Ria the most followed brand on LinkedIn among all its direct competitors.

My role spanned research, scriptwriting, animation, and production, but what I’m most proud of is the strategic coherence of the work. Through videos on immigration, finance and the impact of remittances we were able to reposition Ria as an important player in the fintech ecosystem.

Service Club Delivery

Client: Service Club

Role: Creative Director

2,000 → 14,000 couriers in 6 months+400% Instagram · +75% LinkedInContracts with 4 major platforms

Immersive research uncovered that couriers’ biggest blocker was Spain’s freelancer paperwork. Addressing that helped to grow the courier base 7x and won contracts with Glovo, Uber Eats, Stuart and Getir.

Context

I was hired for a 6 month contract at Service Club Delivery, and while my formal role was as Creative Director, my informal roles included Web Developer, UX/UI Designer, Social Media Manager, UX Researcher and Motion Graphics Designer.

The company was formed to connect couriers to courier companies (Uber Eats, Glovo, Deliveroo, etc), and to reduce their churn rate (which is one of the biggest pain points for these companies). Our services included a job board and an academy where couriers could upskill and earn certificates (think LinkedIn for couriers).

Problem

When I joined SCD I also signed up to be a courier for Deliveroo to better understand the ecosystem, riding around Madrid delivering food for about 3 hours a week. I also interviewed countless couriers, both in person and online through surveys, interviews and data analysis. I soon found the massive pain point that they had, and a way we could fix it.
The hardest part about working for the delivery apps was the infernal pile of indecipherable documents that one had to complete to be able to register as a freelancer. This was made significantly worse by the fact that most couriers are either students or migrants.

What I did

The first step we made was to add a course in the academy on getting and maintaining a Spanish work visa, as well as several others helping people understand what work opportunities were and weren’t available to them. Users had to create a profile on our system to access these courses, which were marketed extensively on online communities that were popular with students and recent migrants. Additionally, we partnered with a company that registered people as freelancers for a small monthly fee (around 12 euros if I remember correctly). This meant that through our interface we were able to offer people a way to fill out this immensely complicated paperwork without lifting a finger.

Results
7xcourier growth

Couriers in the system rose from 2,000 to 14,000 in the six months I worked there. Instagram grew 400% and LinkedIn 75%, and the rapidly inflating metrics won contracts with 4 major companies: Glovo, Uber Eats, Stuart and Getir.

What I learned

The insight that transformed the business came from outside the product. Riding the bike surfaced what surveys alone never would have.

Nomads Relocation

Client: Freelance

Role: AI Automation/Brand Strategist

~5% chatbot conversionNear-zero running costsAutomated lead discovery

A chatbot I built to stop answering friends’ visa questions became a self-sustaining relocation business. AI handles the education and qualification; humans handle the high-stakes legal work.

How it started

Nomads Relocation started as a WhatsApp message. A few friends were planning to move to Spain and kept asking me the same questions about the Digital Nomad Visa, about tax registration, about what paperwork they needed. I built a simple chatbot to handle those questions so I didn’t have to answer them manually every time.

The chatbot was more useful than I expected. When a friend who worked as a real estate agent in the US mentioned that many of her clients relocating to Spain were running into the same friction, I embedded the chatbot in a proper website and started paying attention to the conversations people had with it. The pattern was consistent: once someone had figured out their visa eligibility, they generally asked for an immigration lawyer. A small portion asked about housing, banking and insurance as well. Each service we added came directly from questions the chatbot was already fielding.

How it works

The customer journey now works like this. Most users find us organically, but we also go looking for them. Automated workflows monitor Reddit and Facebook for people mentioning a move to Spain, asking about the Digital Nomad Visa, or expressing frustration with the paperwork process. An AI agent assesses each post for relevance and drafts a response; I review and post it manually, since both platforms penalize automated behavior heavily. The post leads them to the website where they can interact with the chatbot. The chatbot converts at around 5%, and it costs almost nothing to run, and the conversations it has before people are ready to commit are building trust. By the time someone does convert, they’ve already been educated on the process and pre-qualified themselves. The low-touch freemium model also means the business requires very little of my time on a day-to-day basis, which was always the intention.

Hilti

Client: Hilti

Role: UX Researcher

4,000+ emails analyzed200 surveys · 20 interviewsChurn traced to cost confusion, not quality

During my Master’s in Customer Experience & Innovation at IE, I was part of a small team commissioned by Hilti to investigate the drivers behind a high churn rate among lower-income customers. The problem was framed not as a usability issue, but as a mismatch between Hilti’s value proposition and the financial realities of this segment.

Research

We began with large-scale qualitative research, analyzing over 4,000 customer emails to identify recurring themes. From this dataset, approximately 200 customers completed structured questionnaires, and around 20 participated in in-depth interviews. This allowed us to move from anecdotal feedback to validated behavioral patterns.

Findings

Our research revealed that churn was not primarily driven by dissatisfaction with product quality, but by poor understanding of long-term cost structures. Many customers were making purchasing decisions without a clear picture of when renting versus buying made financial sense, leading to regret and disengagement.

Based on these insights, we mapped the customer ecosystem and end-to-end journey for this segment, identifying key decision points where Hilti could intervene earlier. Rather than proposing major changes to the product line, we recommended targeted adjustments to how value was communicated.

The intervention

Our final proposal combined strategic insights with a concrete intervention: a value calculator designed to help customers compare rental and purchase scenarios based on their usage patterns. The goal was to enable more informed decisions upfront.

The brief

The original brief was straightforward: create a clear, bilingual platform for announcing retreats and events, with a booking system that could handle extended stays. Gomde’s audience is roughly 75% German-speaking and 25% English-speaking, so the multilingual setup was a core requirement from the start, as was making the site maintainable by non-technical staff.

Problem

The automation work grew out of my conversations with the staff, many of whom are close personal friends. While I was setting up the booking functionality, I started asking the staff about how they currently managed registrations. The answer was printed calendars and spreadsheets. Every retreat required manual data entry across multiple systems, and attendance information, dietary requirements, and accommodation preferences had to be tracked and communicated by hand. What I built

The system I built works like this: when a staff member creates a retreat or event, a registration form is generated automatically, with dates pre-populated from the event but editable by participants. On submission, attendees receive a confirmation email, are added to the event as Google Calendar attendees, and are enrolled in an automated pre-arrival and post-departure communication sequence. Staff can see at a glance (in Google Calendar) how many people are coming, when, what accommodation they’ve requested, and any dietary requirements. What previously required hours of manual coordination now takes almost no time. Participants are also synced to Mailchimp with language and interest-based tags, replacing the previous approach of sending everyone the same communication regardless of language or program interest.

Kremsegg University

Client: Kremsegg University

Role: Product Designer & Developer

First Buddhist university in AustriaPlain-English site editingAI editor with a GitHub safety net

An early-stage university needed a simple site that non-technical founders could keep changing as the project evolved. So beyond the website itself, I built a password-protected AI editor that lets the client edit any page in plain English — preview, commit to GitHub, then publish.

The context

Kremsegg University aims to be the first Buddhist university in Austria. It’s still in its infancy: the founders have the building and several million euros in investment, and they need several million more to secure the necessary permits. One of the founders approached me to build their website. The intent was modest — a calling card. A place for people interested in the project to learn more, and to donate or invest if they felt inclined.

The problem

I vibe-coded the website. I’d been using Claude to write front-end code for a while and found it reliable, so the build itself went quickly: a donate page, a contact page, and everything else a landing page needs. But the project was moving fast. New donors wanted revisions — wording, photos, small details — and I found myself making constant updates that each took less than 30 seconds to implement but broke my flow every time. It was inefficient for everyone. Worse, I realised I was essentially just copy-pasting the client’s requests into Claude and pushing the result.

The solution

The solution is the reason this otherwise ordinary website is in my portfolio. To kill the constant-updates problem, I built something so simple, so efficient, and so easy to use that I’d be surprised if it doesn’t become commonplace in the next few years. It might even replace traditional CMS platforms altogether.

I added a password-protected AI editor to the site. The client selects a page, describes the change they want in plain English, and can even upload images. A Claude API applies the edit to the site’s code, shows a preview, and only publishes once the client confirms — committing to GitHub first, so nothing is lost if a change breaks something. If a request is too large to handle safely, the assistant offers to email me instead of pushing it.

In other words, the client can now edit their own website just by describing what they want, tailoring it to a shifting audience without friction. They were delighted. I won’t pretend the experience didn’t unsettle me a little — I’d essentially designed myself out of the equation. But I’ve come to believe that evolving your value proposition alongside AI is the only way to avoid being displaced by it.
